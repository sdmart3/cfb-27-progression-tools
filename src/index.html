What a ceiling is

Every player's skill tree is split into six skill groups. A cornerback's are Man Coverage, Zone Coverage, Quickness, Hands, Power and Run Stopping; a quarterback's are different ones. Each group can be upgraded up to 20 levels, so 120 levels is a completely full tree.

When a player is created the game rolls a separate limit for each of his six groups — one player can take Quickness to 18 but Hands only to 9. Those limits are fixed for his whole career. They are not a balance that runs down as he improves; they are the ceiling he is improving toward. However much he plays and however much XP he earns, he stops there.

That ceiling is what this tool changes, plus, in the roster pass, a grant of skill points so a raised ceiling can actually be used. The percentages below are how full a player's tree is allowed to get: 60% means he can reach 72 of the 120 possible levels, and nothing will ever take him past it. It says nothing about how good he is today — a 78 overall senior who is nearly maxed out and a 78 overall freshman with room to spare can have the same rating and completely different futures.

Development Spread: how it works

Every player's six skill-group limits add up to one number out of 120 — his ceiling. The tool rebuilds that number, then splits it back across his six groups in the proportions he already has, so a speed-heavy back stays speed-heavy. Only the size of the ceiling moves, never its shape.

Each player's ceiling is built in seven steps:

  1. Find the average for players like him — same development trait, same position, same class. All three matter. Elite players average a far higher ceiling than Normal ones; ceilings are strongly positional besides, with quarterbacks averaging about 90 of the 120 levels against 74 for offensive linemen; and the classes differ again, because the players who stick around are the ones who had the most ceiling left. So an Elite junior quarterback is measured against other Elite junior quarterbacks. That average is the anchor and it holds still, which is why no trait, position or class comes out stronger or weaker than it went in.
  2. Separate his stars from himself. Within any group of players like him, the game hands higher-star recruits higher ceilings. That part of his distance from the average is his recruiting billing, and it is scaled down rather than stretched. This is Star influence: at 0.5 the ceiling gap between star ratings is halved, so a one-star's future is less settled by his ranking.
  3. Push the rest of him away from that average. Whatever is left, the part that is him rather than his star rating, is stretched: a player above his billing goes further above, one below goes further below. Every star rating gets the same wider range of outcomes. This is Spread.
  4. Add luck. A random nudge up or down, different for every player. This is Randomness.
  5. Let some careers stall. Roster pass only. A blue-chip's ceiling sits so far above anything he reaches in college that no amount of stretching ever stops him. So a small share of players, the same odds at every star rating, draw a bust: their ceiling drops to well below their current overall, and they barely improve from then on. The draw belongs to the player, so a bust stays a bust. This is Busts.
  6. Give the breakouts something to spend. Roster pass only. A higher ceiling on its own does almost nothing: most players never get near theirs, and what holds them back is how many skill points they have to spend. So the players the stretch sends furthest above their star rating's average get 20 skill points each, which the game spends at training results: about +1 overall a season, every season, because a player's luck stays with him. This is Breakout fuel. The league's budget is tied to how much ceiling the pass cut, so growth the cuts take away is handed to the breakouts rather than added on top, and the league's average stays put. It goes by each player's own share of the stretch, not his school, so in a test the recruits who got it were mostly one- to three-stars with hidden upside.
  7. Add his program. Roster pass only. Each player is ranked against others of his own development trait by how well run his school is, so a good program lifts him relative to players of his calibre without lifting his whole tier. This is Program effect.
A player who performs can outrun a bad program. The penalty for playing somewhere poorly run shrinks the further a player stands above his peers — measured by what he actually produced on the field this season, per game and ranked within his own position. The best player in the country at a weak school keeps nearly all of his ceiling; an ordinary player there does not. Players with no snaps on record are judged on their ceiling instead, since they have no season to be read.
Nothing is thrown away. A group cannot pass 20 levels and a player cannot pass 120, but when the maths asks for more it flows somewhere rather than vanishing: past-20 overflow moves into his other groups, and a player pinned at 120 hands the remainder to players like him who still have room. Every group's average is preserved exactly.
Kickers, punters and long snappers sit it out. Their ceilings are untouched, and they are not counted in any number on this page.

How a school's score is built

The roster pass rates every program on four things: the equipment and support staff it has bought (40%), head coach level (25%), facility tier (20%) and coach prestige (15%). What a school has actually paid for counts most; a nice building with no staff and a poor coach counts least.

Each of those four is ranked against every other program before the weights are applied, and the school's final score is ranked too. Ranking matters because early in a dynasty most CPU teams have bought nothing, so scoring them on raw numbers would hand your team a runaway advantage simply for being the only one investing. It also means the weights above are honest: nothing rests on guessing what counts as a "good" coach rating.

A user coach is ranked on the same 1–100 level scale as CPU coaches, by the share of CPU head coaches below him, and capped just short of the top so a maxed-out user is among the best rather than automatically first. A new user coach starts low on this part of the score and climbs as he levels up.

What the Regression Tool changes

Every player carries a development trait: Normal, Impact, Star or Elite. It's the single biggest factor in how fast he improves, and in a normal dynasty it's close to permanent — the label a player is assigned as a recruit is the label he retires with.

This makes it a verdict that gets revisited. A player who has fallen well behind others of his position, class and trait can drop a tier. A player well ahead of them can gain one. Only that one field is ever written. Nobody's ratings move, so nothing changes on the field the moment you run it — what changes is who's still improving in two years and who has quietly stopped.

Regression Tool: how it works

  1. Work out what he should be. A player is measured against the company he keeps: the average overall of players at his position, in his class, with his development trait. A junior Star quarterback sitting at 71 where junior Star quarterbacks average 82 is a career not going the way its billing said it would.
  2. Look at what he actually did. His season is read from the save — snaps, starts and production, scored per game and ranked against others at his position, so a lineman and a quarterback are never compared in the same units. That standing is worth up to eight rating points either way on top of the paper comparison, which is what separates a starter carrying a team from a backup with the same rating. A player with no snaps on record is judged on the paper comparison alone rather than marked down for a season he never had.
  3. Count the trophies. Awards are the hardest evidence in the save that a player produced, and they are weighted by what they are: a Heisman counts far more than a conference player-of-the-week, and preseason selections count for nothing, being predictions rather than results.
  4. Hold him to his billing. Blue-chips start with every advantage: more snaps, bigger numbers, more trophies. So the combined standing is measured against what is typical for players with his position and recruiting star rating. A one-star who outplays other one-stars is a breakout; a five-star merely playing like a five-star is not. Without this, the tool would keep promoting the players recruiting rankings already favoured.
  5. Rank everyone by the whole picture. The further behind that combined standing puts a player, the higher his chance of losing a tier. The bottom of the league is genuinely exposed, the middle barely, the top not at all.
  6. Ask who coaches him. A strong program cuts that chance sharply, a weak one raises it. A player who would have slid at an average school and does not at his is reported as coached up — that is the point of the whole thing.
  7. Let some of those turn all the way around. A share of coached-up players do not merely hold their tier, they gain one: the staff reverses the slide instead of stopping it. Better programs land it more often.
  8. Let the best rise. Players well clear of their peers can gain a tier outright, and a good program makes that likelier too, so coaching is rewarded rather than being only damage control.
  9. Pay out skill points. Being coached up is worth real skill points, so a staff noticing a player was slipping shows up on the player. A turnaround pays half again on top of the tier it carries, a breakout pays half, and every award pays on its own scale regardless of what the roll did. The game spends them at training results; in a test, 10 extra skill points bought about +0.6 overall.
Only the development trait and skill points are written. Ratings, ceilings, positions, injuries and everything else are untouched, so nothing changes on the field the moment the pass runs. What changes is who is still improving in two years.

Before you apply

Go to the main menu or close the game first. If the dynasty is loaded, the game saves its own copy over the changes. The app warns you when the game is running, but it can't tell the main menu from a loaded dynasty.
Your save is backed up automatically before each pass writes, into a Dynasty Backups folder right next to this program. Move the program folder wherever you like and its backups go with it.
Re-running is safe. Each player's original ceiling and the traits he started the season with are remembered, so running again with different settings re-derives from those instead of piling on top. Advancing to a new season starts fresh, so a player who slid last year can slide again this year. Those records live in Documents\CFB 27 Modding\cache.
